# Read

- **The Three-Body Problem** — Liu Cixin (2008). Ideas-first SF at its
  most ambitious; the sociological imagination is the real engine.
- **Diaspora** — Greg Egan (1997). Posthuman ontology done seriously;
  the polis sections are unmatched.
- **Seeing Like a State** — James C. Scott (1998). The *mētis* concept
  is the payoff; first half stronger than second.
- **Death's End** — Liu Cixin (2010). The trilogy closes at cosmological scale;
  the final chapters are the most vertiginous in modern SF.
- **The Dark Forest** — Liu Cixin (2008). The Wallfacer conceit is the trilogy's
  cleverest; game theory rendered as existential dread.
- **The Hunger Games** — Suzanne Collins (2008). YA dystopia that earns its
  darkness; the spectacle-as-politics allegory is sharper than it looks.
- **1984** — George Orwell (1949). The ur-text of political dystopia;
  doublethink has never felt more current.
- **A Brief History of Time** — Stephen Hawking (1988). Cosmology made
  accessible; the ideas outrun the explanations but that's part of the point.
- **Meditations** — Marcus Aurelius (c. 170 CE). A Roman emperor's private
  stoic notebook; the self-discipline is bracing, the honesty disarming.
- **The Complete Robot** — Isaac Asimov (1982). The Three Laws as narrative
  engine; every permutation exhausted with forensic patience.
- **Robots and Empire** — Isaac Asimov (1985). The bridge between the Robot
  and Foundation series; Daneel's purpose goes cosmic.
- **Forward the Foundation** — Isaac Asimov (1993). Seldon's final years,
  posthumously published; elegiac in tone, psychohistory now fully humanised.
- **Foundation and Earth** — Isaac Asimov (1986). Trevize's quest resolves
  but raises bigger questions; Galaxia is the real provocation.
- **Prelude to Foundation** — Isaac Asimov (1988). Seldon before the equations;
  world-building as character study.
- **The Robots of Dawn** — Isaac Asimov (1983). Lije Bailey's last case;
  robot philosophy dressed as a murder mystery.
- **The Naked Sun** — Isaac Asimov (1957). Agoraphobia inverted; a society
  of hermits makes for a neat locked-room twist.
- **The Caves of Steel** — Isaac Asimov (1954). The robot-detective template;
  Elijah and Daneel are one of SF's great odd couples.
- **The Gods Themselves** — Isaac Asimov (1972). Asimov's most formally
  ambitious novel; the alien middle section is unlike anything else he wrote.
- **Foundation's Edge** — Isaac Asimov (1982). The series revived after thirty
  years; Trevize's scepticism drives it more than psychohistory does.
- **Second Foundation** — Isaac Asimov (1953). The hidden foundation revealed;
  the battle of minds is more gripping than any military campaign.
- **Foundation and Empire** — Isaac Asimov (1952). The Mule is the series'
  best creation; a mutant who breaks the plan.
- **A Murder of Quality** — John le Carré (1962). Smiley as detective rather
  than spy; quieter than the Circus novels but the same cold moral atmosphere.
- **Call for the Dead** — John le Carré (1961). Smiley's debut; the Cold War's
  moral ambiguity established in miniature.
- **Inspector Imanishi Investigates** — Seicho Matsumoto (1961). Japanese
  procedural as social novel; the investigation is an excavation of postwar class.
- **The Spy Who Came In from the Cold** — John le Carré (1963). The definitive
  Cold War spy novel; the ending reframes everything that came before.
