## Fiction — Contemporary & General

- **The Remains of the Day** — Kazuo Ishiguro (1989). Repression as English art form; Stevens's unreliable self-accounting is the whole novel.
- **Olive** — Emma Gannon (2022). A novel of female friendship and choice; quiet and emotionally precise.
- **Table for One** — Emma Gannon (2023). Short stories exploring solitude and modern life; compact emotional portraits.
- **The Appeal** — Janice Hallett (2021). An epistolary murder mystery in a theatre company; form and content are inseparable.
- **Ordinary People** — Diana Evans (2018). London domestic lives observed through Obama's election; subtle and precise.
- **Where the Crawdads Sing** — Delia Owens (2018). Southern Gothic mystery; the setting carries more weight than the plot.
- **Pagan Spring** — G. M. Malliet (2013). A village mystery with literary sensibility; Agatha Christie territory.
- **99 Red Balloons** — Elisabeth Carpenter (2017). Two missing children, two mothers; a domestic thriller with a slow-burn reveal.
- **The Help** — Kathryn Stockett (2009). Three women's stories in 1960s Mississippi; commercially polished civil-rights fiction.
- **Us** — David Nicholls (2014). A marriage unravelling on a European tour; comedy that tips into something sadder.
- **The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time** — Mark Haddon (2003). A murder mystery narrated by a neurodiverse teenager; voice and form are its real achievements.
- **The Zahir** — Paulo Coelho (2005). A novelist searches for his missing wife; allegorical in Coelho's typical style.
- **Normal People** — Sally Rooney (2018). Power dynamics in an Irish love story; Rooney's ear for class and ambivalence is exceptional.
- **Conversations with Friends** — Sally Rooney (2017). Two college students in a Dublin affair; cooler and more detached than Normal People.
- **Beautiful World, Where Are You** — Sally Rooney (2021). Four friends navigating meaning and intimacy; the epistolary chapters are the best parts.
- **The Break** — Marian Keyes (2017). A husband takes a six-month break from marriage; Keyes at her funniest and most honest.
- **Again, Rachel** — Marian Keyes (2022). A return to the Walsh family; warm, funny, and structurally inventive.
- **Angels** — Marian Keyes (2002). A Dublin woman goes to Hollywood; early Keyes at its most entertaining.
- **So Lucky** — Dawn O'Porter (2019). Female friendship and ambition in London; O'Porter's sharpest novel.
- **Girl, Woman, Other** — Bernardine Evaristo (2019). Twelve interconnected Black British lives; the Booker Prize was deserved.
- **Miss Smilla's Feeling for Snow** — Peter Høeg (1992). A half-Greenlandic woman investigates a child's death; ice and paranoia.
- **The Hen Who Dreamed She Could Fly** — Sun-mi Hwang (2000). A Korean fable about a hen who wants to hatch her own egg; deceptively simple.
- **The Pig of Happiness** — Edward Monkton (2008). A tiny illustrated philosophy of contentment; more wisdom than its format suggests.
- **Illuminations** — Alan Moore (2022). Short fiction from the creator of Watchmen; baroque and formally ambitious.

- **The Last Devil to Die** — Richard Osman (2023). The fourth Thursday Murder Club; the formula holds.
- **We Solve Murders** — Richard Osman (2024). A new series from Osman; faster-paced than Thursday Murder Club.
- **The House of Silk** — Anthony Horowitz (2011). An authorised Sherlock Holmes continuation; impeccably faithful to the originals.
- **The Word Is Murder** — Anthony Horowitz (2017). Horowitz investigates a murder with a fictional detective; meta and compulsively readable.

- **The Case-Book of Sherlock Holmes** — Arthur Conan Doyle (1927). The final Holmes stories; uneven but with late-period gems.
- **Hannibal** — Thomas Harris (1999). Lecter's sequel to Silence of the Lambs; more baroque and divisive than its predecessor.
- **Hidden Killers** — Lynda La Plante (2014). A young detective faces a serial killer; La Plante's procedural authority is intact.
- **The Lake District Murder** — John Bude (1935). A 1930s detective novel in Cumbria; good regional atmosphere.
- **Murder at the Manor: Country House Mysteries** — British Library Crime Classics (2016). A golden-age anthology; ideal for the genre fan.
- **The Oxford Murders** — Guillermo Martínez (2003). Logic and murder at Oxford; Wittgenstein as plot device.
- **The Secret** — Lee Child & Andrew Child (2024). A Jack Reacher novel; dependable thriller mechanics.
- **State of Fear** — Michael Crichton (2004). A techno-thriller about eco-terrorism; Crichton's most politically contested novel.
- **The Way of All Flesh** — Ambrose Parry (2018). Victorian Edinburgh medical mystery; atmospheric and well-researched.
- **N or M?** — Agatha Christie (1941). Tommy and Tuppence hunt Nazi spies; wartime Christie at her most urgent.
- **The Constant Gardener** — John le Carré (2001). A diplomat's murdered wife leads into a Big Pharma conspiracy; le Carré's moral fury at its sharpest.
- **The Honourable Schoolboy** — John le Carré (1977). Westerby in South-East Asia; sprawling, brilliant, and the Circus at its most morally exhausted.

## Science Fiction & Fantasy

### Isaac Asimov

- **The Complete Robot** — Isaac Asimov (1982). The Three Laws as narrative engine; every permutation exhausted with forensic patience.
- **The Caves of Steel** — Isaac Asimov (1954). The robot-detective template; Elijah and Daneel are one of SF's great odd couples.
- **The Naked Sun** — Isaac Asimov (1957). Agoraphobia inverted; a society of hermits makes for a neat locked-room twist.
- **The Robots of Dawn** — Isaac Asimov (1983). Lije Bailey's last case; robot philosophy dressed as a murder mystery.
- **Robots and Empire** — Isaac Asimov (1985). The bridge between the Robot and Foundation series; Daneel's purpose goes cosmic.
- **Prelude to Foundation** — Isaac Asimov (1988). Seldon before the equations; world-building as character study.
- **Foundation** — Isaac Asimov (1951). The galactic empire begins its fall; psychohistory as civilisational insurance.
- **Foundation and Empire** — Isaac Asimov (1952). The Mule is the series' best creation; a mutant who breaks the plan.
- **Second Foundation** — Isaac Asimov (1953). The hidden foundation revealed; the battle of minds is more gripping than any military campaign.
- **Foundation's Edge** — Isaac Asimov (1982). The series revived after thirty years; Trevize's scepticism drives it more than psychohistory does.
- **Foundation and Earth** — Isaac Asimov (1986). Trevize's quest resolves but raises bigger questions; Galaxia is the real provocation.
- **The Gods Themselves** — Isaac Asimov (1972). Asimov's most formally ambitious novel; the alien middle section is unlike anything else he wrote.
- **The Stars Like Dust** — Isaac Asimov (1951). Early space opera; the plot turns on an unexpected document.
- **The Martian Way** — Isaac Asimov (1955). Short stories from the early Robot/Empire universe; the title story holds up well.

### A Song of Ice and Fire — George R. R. Martin

- **A Game of Thrones** — George R. R. Martin (1996). The political murder-mystery that launched the series; the world-building is the achievement.
- **A Clash of Kings** — George R. R. Martin (1998). The five-kings war begins; darker and more complex than the first.
- **A Storm of Swords 1: Steel and Snow** — George R. R. Martin (2000). The Red Wedding is coming; peak GRRM plotting.
- **A Storm of Swords 2: Blood and Gold** — George R. R. Martin (2000). The aftermath; the series at its most brutal.
- **A Feast for Crows** — George R. R. Martin (2005). Cersei and the South; deliberately slower and more domestic.
- **A Dance with Dragons 1: Dreams and Dust** — George R. R. Martin (2011). Jon, Dany and Tyrion; parallel to Feast, not sequential.

### Discworld — Terry Pratchett

- **The Colour of Magic** — Terry Pratchett (1983). Pratchett's first Discworld; lighter and more satirical than later instalments.
- **The Light Fantastic** — Terry Pratchett (1986). Rincewind continues; the series finds its feet.
- **Equal Rites** — Terry Pratchett (1987). The first Witches novel; Granny Weatherwax arrives.
- **Sourcery** — Terry Pratchett (1988). Rincewind again; the magic arms race as satire.
- **Wyrd Sisters** — Terry Pratchett (1988). The Witches do Macbeth; Pratchett's literary parody at its best.
- **Pyramids** — Terry Pratchett (1989). Ancient Egypt meets the Disc's irreverence; standalone and underrated.
- **Moving Pictures** — Terry Pratchett (1990). Hollywood on the Disc; the talkies as dangerous magic.
- **Jingo** — Terry Pratchett (1997). A Discworld war; Pratchett's sharpest anti-militarism satire.
- **Discworld: The Unseen University Collection** — Terry Pratchett. A companion volume to the Wizards' world.
- **Discworld: The Witches Collection** — Terry Pratchett. A companion to the Witches arc.

### Alastair Reynolds

- **Revelation Space** — Alastair Reynolds (2000). Hard SF on a gothic scale; the Fermi paradox as cosmic horror.
- **Chasm City** — Alastair Reynolds (2001). Noir in Reynolds's decaying future; the city is the real protagonist.
- **Absolution Gap** — Alastair Reynolds (2003). The trilogy closes; cathedrals on a frozen moon.
- **Galactic North** — Alastair Reynolds (2006). Short stories in the Revelation Space universe; the title story spans millennia.

### Harry Potter — J. K. Rowling

- **Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone** — J. K. Rowling (1997). The boy wizard's origin; world-building that rewards rereading.
- **Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets** *(illustrated, Jim Kay)* — J. K. Rowling (1998). Jim Kay's artwork transforms the familiar into something grander.
- **Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban** — J. K. Rowling (1999). The series' pivot point; time-travel and moral complexity arrive together.
- **Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire** — J. K. Rowling (2000). The tournament and Voldemort's return; the series grows up.
- **Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix** — J. K. Rowling (2003). The longest and most political; the Ministry as antagonist.
- **Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows** — J. K. Rowling (2007). The series ends on its own terms; the Hallows mythology is the best late addition.

### Other SF & Fantasy

- **Neuromancer** — William Gibson (1984). The ur-cyberpunk novel; Gibson invented a genre's vocabulary.
- **Dune** — Frank Herbert (1965). Messianic ecology SF; the world-building depth is still unmatched.
- **Ready Player One** — Ernest Cline (2011). Nostalgia as plot engine; the gaming mechanics work better than the prose.
- **The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy: The Trilogy of Four** — Douglas Adams (1979–92). Comic SF that takes ideas seriously; Adams's timing is its own philosophy.
- **The Invisible Life of Addie LaRue** — V. E. Schwab (2020). A woman cursed to be forgotten; the Paris chapters are the finest.
- **Flowers for Algernon** — Daniel Keyes (1966). Intelligence and its costs; the epistolary decline is devastating.
- **His Dark Materials** — Philip Pullman (1995–2000). Oxford, Dust, and free will; the theology deepens with each reread.
- **The Time Machine / The Island of Dr Moreau / The Invisible Man / The War of the Worlds / The First Men in the Moon / The Food of the Gods** *(omnibus)* — H. G. Wells (1895–1904). Six foundational SF texts; Wells invented the template for the next century.
- **Marlfox** — Brian Jacques (1998). A Redwall novel; Brian Jacques's medieval animal world at its most epic.
- **The Chronicles of Narnia: The Magician's Nephew / The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe / The Horse and His Boy** — C. S. Lewis (1950–54). Lewis's allegorical fantasy; the best argument for rereading childhood books as an adult.
- **We** — Yevgeny Zamyatin (1924). The dystopia Orwell and Huxley both drew from; bleaker and stranger than either.
- **The World According to Rick** *(Rick and Morty)*. The show's absurdist philosophy in print form.

## Classics

### Penguin Classics & Classic Literature

- **Candide, or Optimism** — Voltaire (1759). Philosophical satire; the best case for cultivating one's garden.
- **The Story of My Life** — Casanova (1798). The great seducer's autobiography; more interesting for the social history than the conquests.
- **The Prince** — Niccolò Machiavelli (1532). The founding text of political realism; shorter and more nuanced than its reputation.
- **Beyond Good and Evil** — Friedrich Nietzsche (1886). Nietzsche's critique of moral philosophy; more rigorous than Zarathustra.
- **Thus Spoke Zarathustra** — Friedrich Nietzsche (1883). Nietzsche's philosophical prose-poem; the Übermensch as provocation.
- **Frankenstein** — Mary Shelley (1818). The original SF novel; the creature's perspective is more devastating than the monster's.
- **The Republic** — Plato (c. 375 BCE). The ideal city as thought experiment; the cave allegory still resonates.
- **The Last Days of Socrates** — Plato (c. 399 BCE). Socrates's trial, imprisonment and death; the Phaedo is the crown jewel.
- **The Damned** *(Là-Bas)* — J.-K. Huysmans (1891). Satanism in fin-de-siècle Paris; the Decadent movement at its most lurid.
- **Against Nature** *(À Rebours)* — J.-K. Huysmans (1884). The prototype of Decadent fiction; Des Esseintes is the original aesthete-in-retreat.
- **Crime and Punishment** — Fyodor Dostoyevsky (1866). Murder as philosophy; Raskolnikov's psychology is never exhausted.
- **The Woman in White** — Wilkie Collins (1859). The first sensation novel; the double narrative still grips.
- **The Communist Manifesto** — Marx & Engels (1848). The foundational text; more historical and rhetorical than programmatic.
- **The Iliad** — Homer (c. 8th century BCE). Rage, glory and grief at Troy; the greatest war poem.
- **The Art of War** — Sun-Tzu (c. 5th century BCE). Military strategy as philosophy; more useful read metaphorically than literally.
- **Paradise Lost** — John Milton (1667). Satan as the most compelling character in English epic; the theology is the plot.
- **The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde** — Robert Louis Stevenson (1886). The original split-self thriller; shorter and more uncanny than its adaptations suggest.
- **The Nicomachean Ethics** — Aristotle (c. 350 BCE). The good life defined through virtue; *eudaimonia* still the best ethical goal.
- **Dracula** — Bram Stoker (1897). Victorian anxieties dressed as Gothic horror; the epistolary structure is its greatest trick.
- **Prometheus Bound and Other Plays** — Aeschylus (c. 430 BCE). The origins of tragedy; the Oresteia is the summit.
- **The Frogs and Other Plays** — Aristophanes (c. 405 BCE). Ancient comedy that still lands; Dionysus judging dead playwrights is a perfect conceit.
- **Egil's Saga** (c. 1230). One of the great Icelandic sagas; Egil is more psychologically complex than any later Viking archetype.
- **A Tale of Two Cities** — Charles Dickens (1859). The French Revolution as melodrama; the opening and closing lines are Dickens at his most rhetorical.
- **History of the Peloponnesian War** — Thucydides (c. 400 BCE). The first work of analytical history; the Melian Dialogue is still taught in international relations.
- **The Picture of Dorian Gray** — Oscar Wilde (1890). Aestheticism and its moral cost; the portrait is the best single image in Victorian fiction.
- **Tao Te Ching** — Lao Tzu (c. 4th century BCE). Eighty-one verses on the way; its ambiguity is the point.
- **The Count of Monte Cristo** — Alexandre Dumas (1844). Revenge, identity and reinvention at operatic scale; longer than it needs to be, better than it should be.
- **Discourses and Selected Writings** — Epictetus (c. 108 CE). Stoic philosophy from a freed slave; more practical than Marcus Aurelius.
- **The Agricola and the Germania** — Tacitus (c. 98 CE). Rome's encounter with its frontiers; the earliest serious ethnography of Britain.
- **The Complete Odes and Epodes** — Horace (23 BCE). Latin lyric poetry at its peak; *carpe diem* in its original context.
- **Gargantua & Pantagruel** — Rabelais (1532–64). Renaissance humanism at its most excessive; the language is as much the subject as the story.
- **Classical Literary Criticism** — Aristotle / Horace / Longinus (various). The founding texts of Western literary theory; the Poetics is still the most useful.
- **Andromache and Other Plays** — Racine (1667). French neoclassical tragedy; the psychological intensity is unrelenting.
- **The Miser and Other Plays** — Molière (1668). French comedy at its finest; Harpagon is among literature's great misers.
- **A Doll's House and Other Plays** — Henrik Ibsen (1879). Modern drama's founding text; Nora's exit is still radical.
- **The Barber of Seville / The Marriage of Figaro** — Beaumarchais (1775–84). The plays behind two great operas; more subversive than the music suggests.
- **On the Nature of the Universe** — Lucretius (c. 60 BCE). Epicurean atomism in verse; the most beautiful pre-modern argument for materialism.
- **The Koran**. The founding text of Islam; the poetry is inseparable from the theology.
- **The Theban Plays** — Sophocles (c. 441 BCE). Oedipus and Antigone; the roots of western tragedy.
- **The Tempest** — William Shakespeare (1611). The late romance; Prospero's farewell to magic reads as Shakespeare's farewell to the stage.
- **Inferno** — Dante Alighieri (1320). The first canticle of The Divine Comedy; Hell is more vivid than Paradise.

### Penguin Little Black Classics

- **Reflections on the Fall of Rome** — Gibbon (1776). Excerpts from the Decline and Fall; the irony is always the subtext.
- **Meditations of a Solitary Walker** — Rousseau (1782). Late Rousseau; walking as philosophical practice.
- **Lysistrata** — Aristophanes (411 BCE). Sex strike to end a war; the feminist reading was always there.
- **The Rage of Achilles** — Homer (c. 8th century BCE). The opening books of the Iliad; fury and its costs.
- **The Marquise of O—** — Kleist (1808). A short story of honour and violation; unsettling and precise.
- **The Death of Ivan Ilyich** — Tolstoy (1886). A bureaucrat confronts mortality; Tolstoy at his most concentrated.
- **The Education of Frederick Douglass** — Frederick Douglass (1845). Excerpts from the Narrative; reading as the path to freedom.
- **The First Three Circles of Hell** — Dante (1320). The opening of the Inferno; limbo, lust and gluttony.
- **The Murders in the Rue Morgue** — Edgar Allan Poe (1841). The first detective story; Dupin invented the genre.
- **The Death of King Arthur** — Malory (c. 1470). The final chapters of Le Morte d'Arthur; the Round Table falls.
- **Meeting Dr Johnson** — Boswell (1791). Excerpts from the Life; Johnson's conversation is the whole point.
- **Three Sketches from a Hunter's Album** — Turgenev (1852). Russian country life observed with quiet sympathy; atmosphere in miniature.

### Other Classics, Poetry & Myth

- **Meditations** — Marcus Aurelius (c. 170 CE). A Roman emperor's private stoic notebook; the self-discipline is bracing, the honesty disarming.
- **The Divine Comedy** — Dante Alighieri (1320). All three canticles; Paradiso rewards the patience that Inferno doesn't demand.
- **Politics** — Aristotle (c. 350 BCE). The state as natural institution; the classification of constitutions is still useful.
- **Winnie-the-Pooh** — A. A. Milne (1926). The bear of very little brain; a philosophy of contentment in the Hundred Acre Wood.
- **Treasure Island** — Robert Louis Stevenson (1883). The pirate adventure template; Long John Silver is a more complex villain than remembered.
- **The Jungle Books** — Rudyard Kipling (1894–95). Mowgli and the Law of the Jungle; the wolf-pack chapters are underrated.
- **Monkey: A Journey to the West** — Wu Cheng'en *(trans.)* (c. 1592). The Chinese epic about the Buddhist pilgrimage; Sun Wukong is one of literature's great tricksters.
- **To Kill a Mockingbird** — Harper Lee (1960). Race, justice and childhood in 1930s Alabama; Atticus has been complicated but the moral core holds.
- **Fairy Tales** — Hans Christian Andersen (1835–72). The dark originals; sadder and stranger than the Disney versions.
- **Northanger Abbey** — Jane Austen (1817). Gothic parody and social comedy; Austen's most playful novel.
- **The Talisman** — Sir Walter Scott (1825). A Crusades adventure; Scott's medieval romance at its most readable.
- **The Works of W. B. Yeats**. The complete poems; the later political work rewards rereading.
- **The Greek Myths, Vol. 1** — Robert Graves (1955). A comprehensive mythological compendium; Graves's interpretations are idiosyncratic but stimulating.
- **The Greek Myths, Vol. 2** — Robert Graves (1955). The second volume; the annotations are often more interesting than the myths.
- **New Larousse Encyclopedia of Mythology** (1959). The standard reference for world mythology; still useful for breadth.
- **Rediscovering Homer** — Andrew Dalby (2006). The argument that Homer was a woman; revisionist and engaging.
- **The Importance of Being Aisling** — Emer McLysaght & Sarah Breen (2017). Irish comic fiction; the Aisling archetype lovingly skewered.
- **L'homme qui n'était pas mort** — S. Granotier *(French)*. French crime fiction.
