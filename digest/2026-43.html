Week 2026-43

Recommendations for the week of 19 October 2026.

This week rotates the three highbrow edges onto a fresh set: the translated-fiction thread reaches the former Yugoslavia for the first time with Kiš's forged-biography stories of the Stalinist terror; the narrative-nonfiction pick opens a wholly new domain — twentieth-century music — as a history of how art answers to power; and the forensic-nonfiction edge returns with Malcolm's cold, self-implicating essay on the ethics of journalism, wired to the criminology and epistemology-of-evidence interest. The comfort picks hold the three best-loved veins — big-idea SF, cold-weather espionage, Golden-Age crime — while reaching for fresh authors and structures: a crime-SF built on one vertiginous idea about perception, a meticulous Dreyfus-affair thriller, and an inverted Golden-Age puzzle told from the killer's side.

Highbrow

A Tomb for Boris Davidovich — Danilo Kiš (1976)

Seven coldly documentary "stories" trace the lives and engineered deaths of revolutionaries swallowed by the Stalinist terror, written as forged biography — footnoted, cross-referenced, Borgesian — so that fiction and the historical record become impossible to tell apart. Translated literary fiction from the former Yugoslavia, a tradition the shelves have never held: formally strange to its foundations, philosophically serious about history, ideology and the manufacture of truth, and utterly unsentimental; the book that made Kiš's name.

The Rest Is Noise — Alex Ross (2007)

A history of the entire twentieth century told through its music — Strauss and Mahler to Shostakovich under Stalin, Britten, the serialists, Cage and the minimalists — argued as a story about how art answers to power, technology and catastrophe. Narrative nonfiction in a wholly fresh domain (modern classical music), ideas-first and rigorous about the works themselves rather than the gossip; winner of the National Book Critics Circle Award and the Guardian First Book Award.

The Journalist and the Murderer — Janet Malcolm (1990)

Starting from a convicted murderer's lawsuit against the writer who befriended and then damned him, Malcolm builds a forensic, self-implicating essay on the moral confidence trick at the heart of all journalism. Rigorous true-crime-adjacent nonfiction that connects the criminology interest to the epistemology of evidence and betrayal: short, formally reflexive, philosophically serious and coldly unsentimental about its own trade — on the Modern Library's hundred best nonfiction books of the century.

Lowbrow

The City & the City — China Miéville (2009)

A murder investigation in two Mitteleuropean city-states that occupy the very same streets, whose citizens are trained from birth to "unsee" each other, so that crossing between them is the one unforgivable crime. Comfort crime-SF with real conceptual teeth: a police procedural built on a single vertiginous idea about perception and the borders we enforce in our own heads — propulsive, cool, and unsentimental; winner of the Hugo, Arthur C. Clarke, World Fantasy and BSFA awards.

An Officer and a Spy — Robert Harris (2013)

The Dreyfus affair retold through Georges Picquart, the intelligence officer who helped convict Dreyfus and then, appalled to find the real traitor still at large, dismantles the army's cover-up at the cost of his own career. Comfort historical-espionage in the cold, procedural vein already loved, but a fresh author and theatre: meticulously sourced, propulsive, and unsentimental about institutional loyalty — straight to the legal-narrative interest; winner of the Walter Scott Prize and the CWA Ian Fleming Steel Dagger.

The 12.30 from Croydon — Freeman Wills Crofts (1934)

A retired manufacturer dies of cyanide on a flight from Croydon to Paris, and the novel is told almost entirely from the killer's side — his motive, his elaborate plan, his mounting dread — until the quietly relentless Inspector French takes it all apart. Golden-Age crime whose payload is structural and forensic: an inverted mystery built with Crofts's famous slide-rule precision about alibis and timetables, witty and unsentimental, and praised by Dorothy L. Sayers.
