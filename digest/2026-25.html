Week 2026-25

Recommendations for the week of 15 June 2026.

The highbrow picks deliberately spread across three growth edges the recent weeks left untouched: a translated non-Western novel from a region the shelves have never reached (Arabic/Sudanese), narrative ecology beyond Attenborough, and contemporary epistemology on how reasoning actually works. After three weeks tilting toward Latin-American fiction and speculative writing, this widens the aperture rather than deepening one vein. The comfort picks stay in well-loved territory — Discworld, hardboiled crime, idea-driven hard SF — each chosen as a non-owned book that keeps the invariants (a clean idea, no sentimentality) while asking little effort.

Highbrow

Season of Migration to the North — Tayeb Salih (1966)

A formally doubled novel that answers Heart of Darkness back from the colonised side, narrated through an unreliable frame and voted the most important Arabic novel of the twentieth century. It opens the biggest blind spot on the shelves — translated non-Western literary fiction — in a region none of the previous weeks have touched.

The Sixth Extinction: An Unnatural History — Elizabeth Kolbert (2014)

Each chapter turns a single scientific idea — that extinction itself had to be invented as a concept — into a reported story, building a rigorous, unsentimental case rather than an elegy. Narrative ecology that pushes past the Attenborough register into argument-led science writing.

The Enigma of Reason — Hugo Mercier & Dan Sperber (2017)

A precise, counter-intuitive theory: reason evolved not to find truth alone but to argue in groups, so its famous failures are features of a social instrument. Contemporary epistemology that reframes belief and justification, sitting squarely against the Bayesian interest without padding the forensic-statistics shelf.

Lowbrow

Small Gods — Terry Pratchett (1992)

A god reduced to a tortoise with exactly one believer is the cleanest premise Pratchett ever built a book on — belief, power, and authority dissected through comedy. The strongest standalone Discworld and a gap in a deep but incomplete Pratchett shelf.

The Big Sleep — Raymond Chandler (1939)

The plot barely resolves and that is famously not the point; the diction does the work, every line earning its precision. A hardboiled lateral from the cold moral weather of le Carré — stylish, fast, and entirely unsentimental.

The Forever War — Joe Haldeman (1974)

One clean idea — relativistic time dilation that strands a soldier centuries from home with every tour — carries the whole book. Comfort hard SF in the well-covered vein, idea-first and bleak rather than sentimental.
