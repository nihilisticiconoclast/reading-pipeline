Week 2026-22

Recommendations for the week of 25 May 2026.

The highbrow picks push three untouched edges at once: Latin American fiction (the magical-realist source code, not its imitators), literary New Wave SF used as political philosophy, and anthropology that dismantles the standard story of how civilisation began. The lowbrow half leans deliberately into the comfort veins — problem-solving SF, Smiley-era spycraft, and Discworld — picking titles that aren't already on the shelf, so they reward the existing taste without being homework.

Highbrow

Pedro Páramo — Juan Rulfo (1955)

A man walks into a town where everyone is dead and the voices keep talking anyway — the fractured, time-collapsing novel that made García Márquez possible. Short, austere, structurally strange, and the foundational text of the translated Latin American fiction the shelves are missing.

The Dispossessed — Ursula K. Le Guin (1974)

An "ambiguous utopia": a physicist crosses from an anarchist moon to its capitalist twin, and the novel refuses to let either side win. Ideas-first New Wave SF that reads as political philosophy, exactly the register beyond the Golden Age the persona points to.

The Dawn of Everything — David Graeber & David Wengrow (2021)

A 700-page demolition of the "farming gave us hierarchy" story, arguing from the archaeological record that humans have always experimented with how to organise themselves. Rigorous, argumentative anthropology outside the Greece/Rome and World-Wars history already well covered.

Lowbrow

Project Hail Mary — Andy Weir (2021)

A lone astronaut wakes with amnesia on a last-ditch mission to save the sun, and solves his way out one physics problem at a time. Pure page-turning comfort SF — but the engine is competence and ideas, not sentiment, so it still fits the taste.

Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy — John le Carré (1974)

Smiley, hauled out of retirement to hunt a Soviet mole at the top of the Circus. The definitive le Carré and not yet on the shelves — comfort reading in a saturated vein, but the cold moral arithmetic is as serious as ever.

Guards! Guards! — Terry Pratchett (1989)

The Night Watch debut — a secret society summons a dragon and a hungover Sam Vimes has to police a city that doesn't want policing. The best entry point to Discworld's City Watch, and a gap in an otherwise deep Pratchett shelf.
